SpeechSplice: Pronunciation Control in Encoder–Decoder LLM-based TTS by Replacing Encoder Hidden States

Kiyoshi Kurihara, Jumpei Ukita, Yusuke Iwasawa, Yutaka Matsuo

Matsuo Institute, Inc.  /  The University of Tokyo

Method

Most large language model (LLM)-based text-to-speech (TTS) methods offer no explicit function for correcting pronunciation. Consequently, these systems frequently mispronounce many everyday words, such as proper nouns, heteronyms, numerals, and domain-specific terms. While weight-editing or fine-tuning can mitigate this, these solutions risk quality degradation and must operate offline. We propose SpeechSplice, a training-free encoder-output replacement approach for pronunciation control in encoder–decoder LLM-based TTS. SpeechSplice replaces the encoder-output hidden states of a word with those of its intended reading. Cross-architecture and cross-lingual experiments cover ten public TTS systems in 17 model–language conditions. At the hidden state we replace, the intervention moves pronunciation toward the target reading more often when the generator reads the text through cross-attention than when text and speech share one self-attention sequence. In human evaluations, native-language listeners identified speech generated with SpeechSplice as realizing the target reading in 98.6–100.0% of judgments, while a separate study found no significant naturalness difference from unmodified synthesis. By clarifying the conditions under which pronunciation correction succeeds, this study informs the design of future LLM-based TTS architectures capable of pronunciation-controlled speech synthesis.

Quoted verbatim from the Abstract of the paper. No part of this page paraphrases or restates the method in other words.

Audio samples

Every clip below is one of the stimuli that the listeners in the paper's AB selection test actually heard, copied here unchanged. Within each pair, the baseline clip and the SpeechSplice clip were generated from the same input text, the same speaker prompt, the same decoding configuration and the same random seed, so they differ only in the encoder-output splice.

How these items were chosen. For the three proper-name sets we take the first ten items of each 50-item set in item-id order without looking at their outcome; for the Japanese same-surface set we take the lowest-numbered item of each of its ten misreading categories, so that every category is represented.

The last column of each table reports, for that item, whether the majority of its ten listener judgments went to the clip realizing the target — the item-level aggregate of the AB selection test.

Listeners judged which of the two clips realized the displayed target: the orthographic form in English and Chinese, the kana reading alone in Japanese.

Voice prompt (reference speaker)

The file below is a recording of a real human speaker from the JSUT corpus. It is not synthetic speech. It is the voice-cloning prompt behind every Japanese clip on this page — both the proper-name set and the same-surface set — so the voice timbre heard in those synthetic clips is cloned from this single recording.

FileCorpus itemTranscriptFormatDurationProcessing appliedListen
audio/prompt/jsut-basic5000-0011.wav JSUT basic5000 / BASIC5000_0011 木曽川は、しばしば日本のライン川と呼ばれている。 WAV, 16-bit PCM, mono, 48 kHz 3.58 s None. Byte-for-byte identical to the corpus file: no cut, no concatenation, no loudness change, no resampling.

The prompt is kept in its own directory, audio/prompt/, and out of the sample tables above, because the listeners in the paper heard only synthetic clips — never this recording. Mixing the two would misrepresent what was evaluated.

The English and Chinese clips use different prompt speakers, and those two recordings are not redistributed here: English uses a 3.25-second LibriTTS train-clean-100 excerpt, Chinese a BZNSYP excerpt resampled to 44.1 kHz mono. Both corpora are credited below.

Licensing, provenance and attribution

Everything in the sample tables is synthetic speech. Each clip is the output of a frozen T5Gemma-TTS model performing zero-shot voice cloning from a prompt recording. No clip is a recording of a real person saying the sentence next to it, and no speaker on this page uttered, consented to or is aware of these particular sentences. The one exception is the voice prompt in the section above, which is marked as a real recording.
